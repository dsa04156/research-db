## 요약·초록

The global artificial intelligence industry is undergoing an tectonic architectural transition. For the past four years, market capitalization, venture capital flow, and computational resources were heavily concentrated in a frantic race for raw model intelligence. Success was measured in parameter counts, context window sizes, and benchmark dominance. That era is closing. The emergence of highly capable open-weight models—exemplified by recent releases like Thinking Machines Lab’s 975-billion parameter Inkling—has democratized core intelligence. When frontier-level reasoning can be downloaded, hosted locally, and customized for a fraction of the cost of closed APIs, raw cognitive capability ceases to be a scarce commodity. The primary business challenge is no longer teaching a machine how to think. The challenge is establishing exactly where that intelligence is allowed to go, what it is permitted to do, and how to safely control its execution boundaries on physical devices. The AI moat has definitively migrated from the model layer to the Control Plane: Identity, Permissioning, and Execution-Finality. 1. The Interoperability Deadlock: Safety vs. Openness The battleground for this industry shift is currently playing out across global regulatory theaters, most notably between Apple and the European Commission. Under Article 6(7) of the EU’s Digital Markets Act (DMA), dominant "gatekeeper" platforms are legally required to grant third-party applications and alternative AI assistants (such as Google Gemini or Anthropic's Claude) equivalent, interoperable access to deep system features. The EU mandates a decentralized, user-permissioned model: if an end-user grants consent, a third-party AI must be allowed to execute cross-app workflows just like native assistants. Apple has adamantly resisted this requirement on iOS and iPadOS, withholding its upgraded Siri AI from the European market. Their defense hinges on a legitimate architectural vulnerability: opening deep system hooks—allowing an external AI agent to read message databases, modify local files, or execute financial transactions—demolishes the traditional operating system sandbox. In a world of prompt injection vulnerabilities and cloud supply-chain compromises, giving an unvetted autonomous agent direct system-level execution authority creates an unmanageable security attack surface. This political binary—where regulators demand open competition and platform operators claim it is structurally insecure—highlights the exact gap in the current AI stack. Platforms have lacked the specific primitives required to grant interoperability of participation without granting uncontrolled execution authority. 2. Breaking the Binary: The Mechanism of Execution-Finality To resolve this impasse, technical standards programmes are emerging to redefine how operating systems manage autonomous behavior. A primary framework addressing this is an active IETF Internet-Draft submitted by independent inventor Sangam Das: draft-das-execution-finality-ai-interoperability. The architecture moves away from traditional "all-or-nothing" permission models, introducing a hardware-rooted cryptographic sequence that enforces strict boundaries on what an AI can actually accomplish. The Candidate Act and Hold State When an AI assistant (native or third-party) requests an operation configured to produce an external effect (e.g., transmitting data over a network or executing a payment), the operating system immediately intercepts the command. The operation is placed in a non-effective hold state known as a Candidate Act. At this stage, it possesses zero direct authority. The file cannot be exfiltrated, and the packet cannot be sent. B. Hardware-Isolated Predicate Validation While the Candidate Act is held, a Protected Enforcement Domain—such as Apple’s Secure Enclave, an independent Trusted Execution Environment (TEE), or a Data Processing Unit (DPU)—evaluates a set of machine-verifiable authority predicates. This layer independently verifies the application's integrity, current user context (via biometrics), and single-use freshness. C. Scoped Non-Bearer Capabilities and Finality Sinks If validation succeeds, the system authorizes a highly restricted, short-lived scoped non-bearer capability. This capability is cryptographically bound to the specific transaction, the explicit file hash, a time-freshness constraint, and a designated Finality Sink. The Finality Sink resides at the literal hardware or driver boundary of the device (such as the network interface controller, memory-commit interface, or peripheral output). Right before the action takes real-world effect, the Finality Sink executes a mandatory check. If the data payload, destination, or cryptographic token deviates by a single bit from the authorized capability, the system immediately fails closed, trapping the Candidate Act in its non-effective state. 3. Resolving the Multi-Step Workflow Problem A critical limitation of traditional security permissions in an AI economy is user interaction fatigue. If an autonomous agent needs to execute a complex, non-deterministic 20-step workflow—such as coordinating with a localized travel platform, checking a corporate database, purchasing a ticket, and modifying an enterprise calendar—forcing a Face ID or biometric confirmation at every sub-step renders the automation useless. The execution-finality framework resolves this via Bounded Durable Intent Leases. Instead of approving individual technical system calls, the user authorizes a high-level orchestration envelope. This lease cryptographically binds an explicitly defined operational box: a temporal limit (e.g., active for 30 minutes), a resource scope (e.g., access permitted only to specific local databases), and a volume/financial quota (e.g., maximum spend of $500). As the AI agent loops through its multi-step background operations, every single sub-action still generates a fresh Candidate Act. However, the hardware co-processor silently and instantaneously evaluates these acts against the active Durable Intent Lease. If the agent operates within the bounded box, the workflow proceeds fluidly without alerting the user. The moment the agent attempts an unauthorized action outside the lease parameters—such as quietly harvesting contact lists while booking the flight—the scope mismatch triggers an instant, silent fail-closed denial. 4. Multi-Party Governance: Ensuring Platform Neutrality For an architectural framework like execution-finality to satisfy global antitrust regulators, it must solve a glaring geopolitical problem: How do we prevent platform operators from cheating? If Apple or Samsung controls the Secure Enclave and writes the validation rules, they could easily build policies that fast-track their native tools while artificially blocking or lagging third-party competitors under the banner of "security." The IETF specification counteracts this by separating policy enforcement from policy provenance. Security rules are compiled into immutable, cryptographically hashed objects called Policy Bundles. To alter or update a Policy Bundle affecting interoperable AI agents, the architecture mandates multi-party co-signatures. A policy profile becomes valid within the phone's hardware only when authorized by both the platform operator and an independent conformity body or regulatory authority (such as an EU-designated technical auditor). Furthermore, every Everyday Candidate Act evaluation generates a machine-verifiable validation receipt. These receipts can be committed to public or permissioned policy-transparency logs. This allows antitrust regulators, like the European Commission or the Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC), to mathematically audit device performance. By verifying the cryptographic traces, regulators can confirm that first-party and third-party AI requests are treated with absolute parity under the exact same active policy profile. 5. Global Market Crossover: The Sovereign AI Landscape While initiated by the European regulatory deadlock, the transition to hardware-enforced control planes is rapidly expanding to major Asian tech sectors, specifically South Korea (the K-market). The South Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) is aggressively moving forward with its own platform regulations via amendments to the Monopoly Regulation and Fair Trade Act (MRFTA). Simultaneously, South Korea’s technology policy is deeply committed to maintaining "Sovereign AI"—ensuring that local language models like NAVER’s HyperCLOVA X maintain cultural and economic data self-sufficiency against dominant Western hyperscalers. The Candidate Act and execution-finality architecture provides the precise technical bridge South Korea requires. By deploying hardware-isolated execution boundaries at the OS layer, Korean device manufacturers can open their ecosystems to global AI tools to comply with incoming KFTC anti-monopoly mandates.
